\begin{abstract}
Amyloid-like $\beta$-sheet packing spans a wide range of stabilities, from the
kinetically persistent cores of pathological fibrils to contacts that come
apart again, and where a sequence falls in that range decides whether it can
serve as a switchable material. Here, temperature-dependent small- and
wide-angle X-ray scattering of the sequence-defined elastin-like polypeptide
\VPAVG{} resolves a reversible transition between a dispersed low-temperature
state and a hierarchical high-temperature assembly carrying
$\beta$-sheet-like peaks at $Q=1.38$ and $0.72$~\Ainv{} that vanish on
cooling. That recovery is gated by time rather than by temperature alone: on a
continuous cooling ramp none of three chain lengths returns to its heating
branch, while an aliquot held at \SI{6}{\degreeCelsius} between cycles recovers
to within 9\% through seven of them. The assembly is therefore reversible but
slow to undo, which places amyloid-like local order in the role of a physical
junction rather than a terminal aggregate.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

Amyloid-like $\beta$-sheet ordering occupies an unusual position in protein science and polymer materials. Pathological amyloid fibrils formed by proteins and peptides that share no sequence homology are associated with Alzheimer’s disease,\cite{Masters1985-pnas} Parkinson’s disease,\cite{Serpell2000-pnas} type II diabetes,\cite{Cooper1987-pnas} prion diseases,\cite{Pan1993-pnas} and systemic light-chain amyloidosis.\cite{Radamaker2019-ncomms} Many amyloid fibrils are kinetically persistent because extended hydrogen-bonded sheets are reinforced by tightly packed steric-zipper interfaces.\cite{Sawaya2007-nature} Yet amyloid-like order is not intrinsically pathological: functional amyloids store mammalian peptide hormones in pituitary secretory granules\cite{Maji2009-science} and build the extracellular matrix of bacterial biofilms,\cite{Chapman2002-science} and related assemblies are increasingly used as mechanically robust biomaterials.\cite{Knowles2016-am} What differs across these systems is not whether $\beta$-sheet-like order forms but how long it lasts: hormone granules disassemble to release functional monomer, whereas steric-zipper cores are far more persistent. Amyloid-like packing therefore spans a wide range of stabilities rather than committing a sequence to one permanent structure,\cite{Palmadottir2025-bpr} and where a sequence falls in that range is a materials-design variable.

Reversible $\beta$-sheet-like contacts are attractive as candidate physical junctions because backbone hydrogen bonding can stabilize locally ordered assemblies, whereas hydrated or weakly packed interfaces can remain dynamic or reversible.\cite{Kato2012-cell,Hughes2018-science,Luo2018-nsmb,Palmadottir2025-bpr} Elastin-like polypeptides (ELPs) are well suited to test this possibility because their sequence, molecular weight, and lower critical solution temperature (LCST) behavior can be programmed recombinantly.\cite{Meyer2004-zb,Amiram2011-la,Quiroz2015-mh,Varanko2020-ek,Saha2020-jg} Above the LCST, ELPs undergo hydrophobic desolvation and can form coacervates or hierarchical assemblies depending on sequence, concentration, and thermal history.\cite{Varanko2020-ek,Saha2020-jg,Sing2017-zq} In particular, alanine-containing ELPs can form stiff, thermoresponsive gels through arrested phase separation.\cite{Glassman2015-cw,Glassman2016-pq} Although these studies establish the arrested state from time-averaged scattering and rheology, how the assembly evolves toward arrest remains elusive.

\section*{Results and Discussion}

Figure~\ref{fig:static} establishes the low- and high-temperature structures and their reversibility. Two aliquots from the same stock were measured at APS 12-ID-B by simultaneous SAXS/WAXS at \SI{13.3}{\kilo\electronvolt}.\cite{APS12IDB,Xu2022-12IDB,Zheng2022-12IDB} The ``Measurement'' aliquot had undergone more than 10 heating/cooling cycles; the ``Reference'' aliquot had stayed at \SI{4}{\degreeCelsius}. At \SI{10}{\degreeCelsius} the two overlap, and a Guinier fit to the Reference gives an apparent $R_g=22.65\pm0.50$~\AA{} (SI Appendix), a dispersed state with no mesoscale aggregates down to $Q=0.0081$~\Ainv, which corresponds to structures below approximately \SI{78}{\nano\meter}. At \SI{30}{\degreeCelsius}, both aliquots reproducibly develop an approximate $I(Q)\propto Q^{-3.2}$ response at low $Q$ (slopes $-3.17$ and $-3.15$ over $0.012<Q<0.040$~\Ainv). Both also develop the same pair of high-$Q$ features, at $Q=1.38$ and $0.72$~\Ainv{} in the Reference and 1.36 and 0.74~\Ainv{} in the Measurement. The cycled aliquot alone makes the point: it still develops both peaks at \SI{30}{\degreeCelsius} and shows neither at \SI{10}{\degreeCelsius}. A more direct test at 8-ID-I took a single aliquot through seven consecutive \SIrange{6}{34}{\degreeCelsius} cycles, heated at \SI{1}{\degreeCelsius\per\minute} (SI Appendix). Across the seven cycles the \SI{6}{\degreeCelsius} profile recovers to within a 9\% relative standard deviation with no systematic drift, and at \SI{33.8}{\degreeCelsius} the correlation function stays close to $1+\beta$ in every cycle, the signature of an arrested state. The sample therefore assembled and arrested on every heating and returned to the same low-temperature state on every cooling (SI Appendix). Video~S1 independently shows recovery of optical transparency after cooling.

The two reciprocal-space regimes describe a hierarchical assembly. Power-law small-angle scattering signifies density heterogeneity extending across the probed interval rather than a single resolved structural scale.\cite{Teixeira1988-jac} Related gel-forming ELPs exhibit disordered bicontinuous or fractal protein-rich networks produced by arrested phase separation.\cite{Glassman2015-cw,Glassman2016-pq} The 4.5 and 8.8~\AA{} spacings are compatible with strand-to-strand and sheet-to-sheet packing in cross-$\beta$-like assemblies, whose reflections are reported at approximately 4.8~\AA{} along the fibril direction and between 8 and 11~\AA{} across it.\cite{Sawaya2007-nature,Sunde1997-jmb} Both features are broad and exhibit full widths at half maximum of 0.25 and 0.34~\Ainv{} (correlation lengths $2\pi/\Delta Q$ of only about 25 and 19~\AA), so the ordering extends over a few repeat spacings rather than forming extended cross-$\beta$ crystallites. Their concurrent appearance with the low-$Q$ response establishes coexistence of local ordered contacts and mesoscale density heterogeneity. The disappearance of the peaks on cooling identifies thermally labile $\beta$-sheet-like associations that are plausible, rather than uniquely established, physical junctions.


The comparison of Figure~\ref{fig:static} sets two separate 12-ID-B aliquots against each other. The test in Figure~\ref{fig:cycle} is more direct: one aliquot at 8-ID-I, taken through seven consecutive cycles and compared with itself, so that any difference between cycles can only be irreversibility. The aliquot is a separate loading of the same stock, measured in the 2022-1 beamtime. Each cycle held the sample at \SI{6}{\degreeCelsius}, ramped to \SI{34}{\degreeCelsius} at \SI{1.01}{\degreeCelsius\per\minute} over 270 consecutive \SI{2}{\second} acquisitions, and returned to \SI{6}{\degreeCelsius}, taking \SI{5.8}{\hour} in total. No acquisitions were taken during cooling, so the cooling segments of Figure~\ref{fig:cycle}a are a projection from the fixed control protocol rather than a measurement; every solid segment is measured, once per acquisition, from the RTD of the stage zone holding the cell.

Three states are sampled in every cycle: \SI{6}{\degreeCelsius}, and two windows near the top of each ramp at \SI{31.9}{\degreeCelsius} and \SI{33.8}{\degreeCelsius}. Averaged over $0.004<Q<0.008$~\Ainv, the seven cycles agree to a relative standard deviation of 9\% at \SI{6}{\degreeCelsius} and 5\% at \SI{33.8}{\degreeCelsius}, with no drift from the first cycle to the seventh. At \SI{31.9}{\degreeCelsius} the spread is 33\%, but that reflects where on the ramp each cycle happened to be sampled rather than a difference between cycles: the windows fall between \SI{31.62}{\degreeCelsius} and \SI{32.48}{\degreeCelsius}, which is on the steep part of the transition. Regressing the band-averaged $\ln I$ of each cycle on that cycle's mean window temperature gives $\mathrm{d}\ln I/\mathrm{d}T=\SI{0.95}{\per\degreeCelsius}$ with $r=0.974$, and the spread taken about that line falls to 7\%. The same regression at \SI{33.8}{\degreeCelsius}, past the steep part, takes 5\% to 1\%. The cycles therefore repeat to better than 10\% at every state once the sampling temperature is accounted for.

The correlation functions say what state the sample was in when each was taken (Figure~\ref{fig:cycle}c). At \SI{33.8}{\degreeCelsius} the correlation function shows no resolvable decay anywhere in the accessible window, from $10^{-2}$ to \SI{2}{\second}, in any of the seven cycles: the sample was arrested on every heating. At \SI{31.9}{\degreeCelsius} it decays throughout that same window, so the assembly is still mobile \SI{1.9}{\degreeCelsius} lower. The dashed line in that panel is the measured correlation level of a static reference recorded in the same beamtime, read at the same $Q$ bin. A static sample cannot relax, so its level is the instrumental contrast, and showing it as a curve rather than as a fitted number keeps the comparison between two measurements. The \SI{33.8}{\degreeCelsius} correlation functions sit just below it and the \SI{31.9}{\degreeCelsius} ones fall well away from it. A contrast value is needed to fit a correlation function, which is why a fitted series carries its own calibration; it is not needed to judge whether a sample has arrested, which is what this panel does, so no arrested fraction is quoted from it. No correlation function is shown at \SI{6}{\degreeCelsius}, where the coherent signal at the lowest $Q$ is too weak for any contrast to be resolved.

Whether the assembly comes apart again is a question about rate as much as
about temperature. Three chain lengths, \VPAVG{} and its twenty- and
ten-repeat counterparts, were taken from \SI{20}{\degreeCelsius} up and
immediately back down on one stage, each reaching a different ceiling
because one protocol meets three different phase diagrams: 45.2, 41.4 and
\SI{33.1}{\degreeCelsius} for ten, twenty and thirty repeats
(Figure~\ref{fig:ramps}). On the way up all three build low-$Q$ intensity and
all three lose the decay of $g_2$, so all three assemble and arrest.

None of them comes back down the way it went up. Averaged over
$0.004<Q<0.008$~\Ainv, the cooling branch at \SI{25}{\degreeCelsius} and below
stands 8.4, 15.7 and 3.6 times the heating branch for ten, twenty and thirty
repeats, while at the top of each ramp the two branches agree to within 2 to
19\%. The hysteresis is therefore in the cold state, not in the hot one: every
sample reaches the same assembled state it would on heating, and none of them
has finished dissolving by the time the ramp returns.

That is a statement about the cooling rate rather than about reversibility.
The ramp spends about twenty-five minutes between the ceiling and
\SI{20}{\degreeCelsius}, and the hysteresis does not order with chain length,
which a sequence-encoded irreversibility would. Given time at low temperature
the same material does recover: the aliquot of Figure~\ref{fig:cycle},
measured in a separate beamtime and held at \SI{6}{\degreeCelsius} between
cycles, returns to the same \SI{6}{\degreeCelsius} profile to within a 9\%
relative standard deviation through seven heating and cooling cycles, with no
drift from the first to the seventh. The two measurements are consistent and
they bracket the answer: the transition is reversible, and undoing it is
slower than building it.
